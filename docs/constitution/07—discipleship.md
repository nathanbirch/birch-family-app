# The Birch Family Constitution

# Discipleship

> "If ye love me, keep my commandments."
>
> — John 14:15

There are many good things a person can become — intelligent, successful,
talented, respected, influential, generous. Every one of those is a worthy
pursuit, and we would be pleased to see any of them in our children. Above all of
them, we hope to become disciples of Jesus Christ, and everything else in this
Constitution grows out of that one desire.

The distinction matters more than it might appear. We do not love truth because
honesty is a good policy; we love it because Jesus is "the way, the truth, and
the life." We do not forgive because forgiveness is emotionally healthy, though
it is; we forgive because we have been forgiven. We do not serve because service
looks admirable; we serve because the Savior "went about doing good." This family
does not follow principles because they work. We follow them because they make us
more like Him. When a principle stops being effective, we intend to keep
following it anyway.

---

The word *disciple* comes from the idea of a learner, which means a disciple is
not somebody who has arrived. A disciple is somebody who has decided whom to
follow. Discipleship is therefore much less about perfection than about
direction.

All of us will have days when we fall short. We will lose our tempers, speak
before thinking, disappoint one another, and make promises we then struggle to
keep. We will sin. None of that means anybody has stopped being a disciple; it
means we need the Savior daily, which was always the arrangement. The measure of
discipleship is not whether we stumble but whether we keep turning back toward
Jesus Christ afterward.

---

The Savior never invited anybody merely to admire Him. He invited them to follow
Him, which means letting His life reshape ours until we are asking different
questions than we used to. Not only "what do I want" but "what would He want me
to become." Not only "what can I get" but "what can I give." Not only "what is
easiest" but "what is right." Those questions change a person slowly. Over years,
our desires start to resemble His, and that transformation is one of the greatest
miracles the gospel offers.

---

Discipleship begins at home, and long before the world gets to see anyone's
character, this family experiences it. The way we speak to a sibling matters. The
way we respond when Mommy or Daddy asks for help matters. The way we treat each
other when nobody outside the house would ever find out matters most of all,
because it is possible to be perfectly kind in public and impatient at the
kitchen table, and everyone here knows it. Jesus never taught that version of
discipleship. He taught integrity — being the same person in private that we hope
to be seen as in public. That kind of consistency cannot be manufactured on
demand. It grows one decision at a time.

---

A disciple is teachable, because the moment we believe we already know enough we
stop growing. We hope this is always a family of learners: people who ask
questions, study, seek wisdom, listen properly, and are willing to change their
minds when truth requires it. Being corrected is not evidence of failure; it is
frequently one of God's better gifts, badly disguised. A humble heart can learn
almost anything, while a proud one struggles with simple truths because it is too
busy defending what it already thinks.

A disciple is also courageous, because following Jesus Christ is not consistently
easy. Sometimes it means standing alone, sometimes being misunderstood, sometimes
saying "I'm sorry" and sometimes saying "no." Most often it means quietly doing
the right thing when nobody notices and there is no reward for it. Real courage
is rarely loud. It usually looks like choosing honesty over convenience, purity
over popularity, service over recognition, and faith over fear — choices that
seem small individually and, over a lifetime, turn out to have been the whole
thing.

And a disciple is joyful, not because life is easy but because hope is always
available. Jesus Christ never promised His followers would avoid hardship. He
promised they would never face it alone, and that promise changes the character
of grief, disappointment, and uncertainty without pretending any of them away.
Joy is not insisting that everything is fine. It is trusting that God is still
good while things are not.

---

One of the real mercies of discipleship is that nobody is expected to become like
Jesus Christ on their own. He gave us parents and siblings, prophets and
scriptures, the Holy Ghost, the Church, friends, teachers, and neighbors, and
each one shapes us. Just as importantly, it works in the other direction: every
encouraging word, every act of forgiveness, every testimony shared, every prayer
offered, and every quiet act of service moves somebody else a step closer to the
Savior. That is holy work, and any Birch of any age can do it this afternoon.

---

One day each of us will stand before Jesus Christ. We do not imagine He will
begin by asking what we earned, how well known we became, or what we collected
along the way. We imagine He will look at our hearts and ask whether we loved
Him, whether we loved His children, and whether we became a little more like Him.
That is the life we are trying to live — not to earn heaven, which is not on
offer at any price, but because we love the One who made it possible.

If the Birch family ends up known for anything, we hope it is this: we followed
Jesus Christ, imperfectly and faithfully, together.
