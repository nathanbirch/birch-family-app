# The Birch Family Constitution

# Building Zion at Home

> "And the Lord called his people Zion, because they were of one heart and one
> mind, and dwelt in righteousness; and there was no poor among them."
>
> — Moses 7:18

When the Lord describes Zion, He does not start with buildings or programs or
accomplishments. He starts with people — of one heart, of one mind, dwelling in
righteousness, and caring for each other so thoroughly that there was no poor
among them. Whatever else Zion is, it is first a description of how a group of
people treat one another.

For most of our lives we thought of Zion as something large and eventual: a city,
a gathering, a future condition of the Saints. We still believe that. We have
also come to believe it begins much smaller than that, around a dinner table, in
a living room, in carpools and bedtime prayers and family councils and entirely
ordinary Tuesdays. Before we can help build Zion anywhere else, we would like to
build it here. That, underneath everything, is what this Constitution is about —
not creating a perfect family, but creating one that is slowly learning to be of
one heart.

---

A Zion home is not a home without disagreement, without tears, or without a
parent who occasionally loses their temper and has to come back and apologize
for it. If that were the definition, no family on earth would ever qualify, and
the word would be useless. What makes a home Zion is the direction it is facing
together.

Every day this house gets a little more selfish or a little more selfless, a
little more hurried or a little more peaceful, a little more worldly or a little
more centered on Jesus Christ. The choices that decide which are almost always
too small to feel important at the time: a soft answer instead of a sharp one, a
sincere apology instead of a stubborn defense, a prayer offered by somebody who
did not feel like praying, an invitation to join the game, a note left on a
pillow, one more story, one more chance, one more act of forgiveness. Zion is
built out of ordinary moments with an unreasonable amount of love in them.

---

Our two mottoes describe the work from both ends. **Love Like Jesus** governs
every relationship in this house, because asking how the Savior would treat the
person in front of us tends to answer the question immediately. Would He listen?
Would He notice? Would He tell the truth kindly, and choose patience over
irritation? The answer is nearly always yes, and we nearly always already knew
it. The more this home resembles the way Jesus treated people, the more it
resembles Zion.

**Think Celestial** governs the other end, because families are easily trapped by
the urgency of the present moment. A spilled drink feels enormous. A messy
bedroom feels unbearable. A sibling argument at seven in the evening can feel
like a genuine emergency. Thinking celestially asks a different question of that
moment — will this matter in a hundred years, and will the way I respond help
this child become who God made them to become? Sometimes the problem genuinely
does need solving. Sometimes the child just needs a parent who sees them. The
person comes before the problem.

---

We do not expect our children to turn out perfect, and we would not know what to
do with them if they did. We hope they turn out honest, courageous, humble enough
to apologize, strong enough to forgive, curious enough to keep learning, and
faithful enough to keep walking. We hope they become people others instinctively
trust, who make a room warmer by entering it, who notice the lonely child before
anybody else does, who keep their promises, who work hard without needing
applause, and who bless the one. If that happens, we are entirely unworried about
whether they are also successful.

---

Building Zion means building a home where grace is easier to find than shame.
There will be broken dishes and broken rules, broken promises and occasionally
broken hearts. None of that surprises God, and it should not surprise us either.
Repentance is not the emergency procedure for disciples who have failed; it is
the ordinary daily path of every disciple there has ever been.

So we want our children never to be afraid of admitting they were wrong. They
should know they can tell us the uncomfortable truth, that confession is met with
love before it is met with consequences, and that the consequences exist to teach
rather than to humiliate. We want this house to sound more like the Savior than
like the accuser. That is not the same as excusing wrongdoing — it means
remembering, in the middle of correcting somebody, that we are correcting a
person who is infinitely loved by Heavenly Father.

---

Zion also takes work, and not only the kind that appears on a chore chart. It
takes work to forgive, to speak gently when tired, to keep learning, to serve
without being noticed, to repent, to pray when heaven seems quiet, and to keep
covenants through decades of ordinary years that will never make the news. That
is holy work, and this family will never measure itself only by what it
accomplishes. We will also ask who we were becoming while we accomplished it,
because character lasts longer than achievement and love lasts longer than
either.

---

We hope this becomes the kind of house where friends feel welcomed before they
feel impressed and where neighbors know they can knock. Where laughter is common
and scriptures are open and prayer is unremarkable. Where forgiveness is quick,
kindness is ordinary, children know they are loved before they have earned
anything, and parents are willing to say "I was wrong."

If our children someday remember very little about the furniture, the vacations,
or the size of the house, we will not be disappointed. We would much rather they
remember how it felt — peaceful, honest, safe, and full of laughter, and above
all like a place where Jesus Christ would have been comfortable sitting at the
table. That is the home we are trying to build. Not perfectly, and not quickly,
but patiently, one apology and one ordinary day at a time.

> "Charity never faileth."
>
> — Moroni 7:46
