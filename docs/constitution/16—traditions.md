# The Birch Family Constitution

# Traditions

> "Remember, remember that it is upon the rock of our Redeemer that ye must build
> your foundation."
>
> — Helaman 5:12

Families are built out of ordinary days. Very little of a life is made of
milestones; most of it is Tuesday dinners, Saturday chores, birthday breakfasts,
bedtime prayers, scripture study before school, family movie nights, long drives,
inside jokes, and walks around the neighborhood. The routines that repeat year
after year are the things that eventually turn into a childhood.

That is why we treasure traditions. They are more than habits — they are repeated
reminders of what matters, and they teach values long before a child is old
enough to explain a value in words. A family tradition is a way of saying *this
is who we are* often enough that nobody has to be told.

---

Some of our traditions are ancient and shared with the whole Church: the
sacrament, family prayer, scripture study, and the celebration of the Savior's
birth and resurrection. Others are entirely ours — birthday pancakes, Christmas
pajamas, game nights, camping trips, the way the app tells everyone whose turn it
is to sit where, the phrases we repeat around the dinner table, the way we welcome
somebody home, and the way we comfort each other after a hard day. None of them
happened by accident. Together they make the rhythm of this house.

One of the better gifts parents can give children is consistency, which is not
the same as perfection and is a great deal more achievable. Children flourish
when they know what kind of home they are coming back to: one where prayers are
offered and scriptures are opened, where laughter is common and people apologize,
where birthdays are celebrated and work is expected, and where Jesus Christ is
remembered. Those repeated experiences quietly become part of who a child is, and
long after they have left home they tend to find themselves recreating them. That
is one of God's more elegant arrangements — faith passed down through ordinary
repetition.

---

We celebrate Christmas with a great deal of joy, and we celebrate it because
Jesus Christ came into the world and changed every other story ever told. Around
that we keep the family traditions our children look forward to all year. We
believe in the joy of Santa Claus — not because Christmas depends on him, but
because wonder is one of God's gifts to children and we have no intention of
being in a hurry about it. The excitement of Christmas morning, the cookies left
on a plate, the note, the full stocking, the look on a child's face: those become
part of the story of a childhood.

One day our children will understand these traditions differently than they do
now. When that day comes, we hope what they remember is not a deception but a
season full of generosity, imagination, anticipation, and love. The tradition was
never about convincing anybody of anything. It was about giving them joyful
memories pointing toward the greatest gift ever given.

Easter gets the same treatment and the same gratitude. The Easter Bunny, the egg
hunts, the baskets, and the spring weather are all happy parts of how we
celebrate — and Easter itself belongs to the risen Savior. Every spring we
remember that death was overcome, that the tomb was empty, and that because He
lives, families can be forever. No tradition we keep will ever mean more than
that one.

The smaller traditions carry their own weight. The Tooth Fairy makes growing up
exciting. Halloween gets us laughing together. Birthdays insist that every life
is worth celebrating. Anniversaries honor covenants, and family reunions remind
everyone that this story is bigger than any of us. We hope our children look
forward to all of it — less for what they receive and more for who is in the room
when they receive it.

---

Birthdays deserve a particular mention here, because each one is a reminder that
God sent somebody irreplaceable into this family. No two people contribute in the
same way, and a birthday is the day we say so out loud. We would rather they
become days of gratitude than days of receiving: a handwritten note, a favorite
meal, an act of service, words of encouragement, and a good deal of laughter tend
to outlast anything expensive. Love is remembered long after the wrapping paper
has gone out with the recycling.

---

As the family grows, the traditions will grow too. Some will change and new ones
will appear; children will marry, grandchildren will arrive, homes will move, and
circumstances will do what circumstances do. That is natural and it is fine.
Traditions exist to serve people, and people should never end up serving
traditions. When one stops helping us love God or love each other, we should have
the courage to change it, because the purpose of every tradition here is the same
— to bring us closer to Jesus Christ and to one another.

The one we most hope survives every generation is simply gathering. Technology
has made staying in touch easier than it has ever been and it has not replaced
being in the same room: sharing a meal, playing a game, talking late into the
evening, watching cousins turn into friends, praying together, and laughing until
somebody cries. Families do not become strong mainly through grand events. They
become strong through thousands of unremarkable moments spent in each other's
company.

---

If future generations remember the Birch family, we hope they remember more than
what we did each December. We hope they remember what those traditions were
teaching — that Jesus Christ is first, that people matter more than possessions,
that kindness is stronger than pride, that service brings joy and repentance
brings hope, that work is honorable and truth matters, that laughter belongs in a
righteous home, and that love is something chosen daily rather than felt
occasionally. If our traditions taught that, they did their job.

May every celebration we hold and every ordinary evening we spend together point
these hearts quietly toward heaven. Traditions fade, decorations wear out, and
children grow up and leave. A home centered on Jesus Christ goes on blessing
people for generations.
