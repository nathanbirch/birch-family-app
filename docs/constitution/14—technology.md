# The Birch Family Constitution

# Technology

> "See that all these things are done in wisdom and order."
>
> — Mosiah 4:27

We live in a remarkable time. Never before have ordinary people carried this much
knowledge, communication, and creative capability around in a pocket. With one
device a person can read the scriptures, talk to somebody on the other side of
the world, learn a skill, make art, study history, write software, compose music,
and stumble across ideas that previous generations could not have reached in a
lifetime of trying.

Technology is one of the genuine blessings of this era, and like every powerful
gift it has to be handled with some care. This family does not believe technology
is the enemy, and we have no interest in pretending we can raise children outside
of it. Neither do we intend to let it run the house. We would like to use it
deliberately, gratefully, and on purpose.

---

Every tool shapes the person using it. A hammer builds a home or wrecks one; fire
warms a family or takes a forest. Technology is no different — it amplifies
whatever we bring to it. It can deepen friendships or quietly replace them,
strengthen a testimony or crowd it out, teach truth or spread nonsense, enable
meaningful work or consume an entire evening without producing anything at all.

So the useful question is almost never whether a given technology is good or bad.
The better question, and the one we hope every Birch learns to ask, is **who am I
becoming because of the way I use it?**

---

**Think Celestial** applies to screens as much as to anything else. Before
opening an app, starting a video, or scrolling a feed, it is worth asking whether
this will help us become more like Jesus Christ, strengthen a relationship, teach
us something worthwhile, or leave us glad we spent the time that way.

Not every moment has to be productive, and we want to be clear about that. God
made beauty, laughter, recreation, and play, and rest is not a failure of
discipline. But mindless distraction has a way of quietly taking things we never
agreed to give it. Minutes become hours, conversations become interruptions,
family dinners go silent, prayers get rushed, and the people sitting beside us
become less interesting than people we will never meet. Technology should enrich
real life. The moment it starts substituting for it, something has gone wrong.

---

One of the best things a device can do is help somebody learn, and we would like
this family to be known for building with technology rather than only consuming
it. Asking real questions, reading great books, studying scripture, working out
how something functions, solving a problem, writing, composing, inventing,
making. God gave us minds capable of extraordinary creativity, and the right tool
should expand that rather than sedate it. Wherever we can, we would rather be
makers than an audience.

---

Artificial intelligence is a good example of the whole argument. Used well it is
an extraordinary tutor, brainstorming partner, translator, and editor. It can
help a person think more clearly, surface ideas they had not considered, and take
enough drudgery off a task to leave time for people.

It is also not wisdom. It is not revelation, it is not conscience, and it is
certainly not the Holy Ghost. It misunderstands questions, misses context, and
states incorrect things with total confidence, which is a genuinely dangerous
combination for anybody who has stopped checking. So we do not ask it to replace
our judgment. We use it to sharpen our thinking rather than to hand our thinking
over. Important decisions belong in prayer, in scripture, in counsel with people
who know and love us, and in honest reflection. A machine can inform that
process. It must never become the voice that replaces God, and it is not
permitted to become a substitute for asking a parent.

---

Relationships deserve undivided attention, and there is a real difference between
being in the room and being present in it. A child telling a story deserves eyes
that look up. A spouse deserves more than distracted listening. A friend deserves
more than half of us. Love requires presence, and some of the most important
moments in a family arrive without warning — a conversation before bed, a walk, a
shared laugh, a difficult confession, a hug from somebody who does not normally
initiate one. If our attention is permanently elsewhere, those are precisely the
moments we will miss. When people in this house need us, they should never have
to compete with a screen.

---

Technology also requires self-control, partly because a great deal of it is
engineered specifically to prevent that. Many digital experiences are designed to
hold attention as long as possible, rewarding constant checking and refreshing
until a mind starts expecting continuous stimulation and finds quiet unbearable.

Discipleship runs on a different rhythm — silence, stillness, scripture, prayer,
deep work, real conversation, reflection. Those things grow in quiet places, and
we intend to protect the quiet places. Not because we are against technology, but
because of what silence lets a person hear. The Holy Ghost is described as still
and small far more often than loud and urgent, and a permanently distracted life
makes that voice very difficult to pick out.

---

We hope our children never measure themselves by likes, followers, views, scores,
or rankings. Those numbers change daily and identity does not. They are beloved
children of Heavenly Parents, and nothing on the internet can add to or subtract
from that. Learning to want God's approval more than the approval of strangers
turns out to be enormously freeing, and we hope they find that out young.

As a family we will put the devices down on purpose sometimes — not because
technology is wicked but because relationships are better. We will read together,
play games, go outside, work with our hands, visit neighbors, laugh around the
table, sit quietly with the scriptures, talk, listen, and pray. Some of the
richest things in a life cannot be downloaded. They have to be lived.

---

We do not particularly hope that future Birches become experts in technology.
Technology will keep changing; new devices will arrive and today's will look
absurd. The principles should hold. Use every tool to bless people. Protect your
attention. Seek truth. Create more than you consume. Think carefully, pray often,
and love people more than screens. Whenever a device offers a choice between
convenience and discipleship, choose discipleship — because no invention, however
remarkable, is ever going to improve on the two great commandments. Everything
else is just another way to practice them.
