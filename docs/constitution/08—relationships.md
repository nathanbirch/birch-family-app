# The Birch Family Constitution

# Relationships

> "By this shall all men know that ye are my disciples, if ye have love one to
> another."
>
> — John 13:35

Every life is built on relationships. Long after the grades are forgotten, the
careers have changed, and the possessions have worn out, the people we loved and
the way we loved them will still be there. God could have built His plan around
almost anything and He built it around relationships — ours with Him, with our
families, with our neighbors, with the people who are easy to love and the people
who are not. That choice tells us something about what He considers important.

So in this family we would like to become unusually good at loving people. Not
because it comes naturally, which for most of us it does not, but because it is
what the Savior did.

---

Love is easily misunderstood. The world often treats it as a feeling, and
feelings matter — but they come and go, frequently at inconvenient moments, and
a family built on them alone would be a precarious thing. The love Jesus Christ
demonstrated was deeper than that, and it was mostly a series of decisions. He
chose to stop for the blind man, to eat with sinners, to forgive His enemies, to
wash the feet of His disciples, and to stay on the cross. Real love is something
we repeatedly choose: often when it is inconvenient, sometimes when it is costly,
always because another person matters.

---

The first thing we hope every Birch child learns is this: **the person comes
before the problem.**

There will always be problems. Broken toys, broken dishes, broken schedules,
broken plans, and occasionally broken trust. Those things are real and some of
them matter a great deal. None of them matter more than the person standing in
front of us, because people are eternal and problems generally are not.

What this looks like in practice is a pause — before reacting, before correcting,
before getting frustrated, before leaping to fix the situation. Long enough to
remember that whoever is in front of us is worth more than whatever has gone
wrong. It is a small habit and it has the power to change the temperature of an
entire house.

---

Relationships flourish where people feel safe, and it is worth being precise
about what kind of safety we mean. Not safety from correction — a family that
never corrects anyone is not being kind, it is being absent. Safety from
rejection. A healthy family tells the truth, apologizes, corrects, teaches, sets
boundaries, and keeps promises, while making one thing completely unmistakable:
*you are loved here.* Not because you earned it, not because you behaved, and not
because you have never disappointed us. Because you belong. There should never be
a day in this house when a Birch has to wonder about that.

---

Kindness is one of the great underrated forms of strength. Anyone can be
sarcastic, anyone can win an argument, and anyone can identify what is wrong with
somebody else. It takes considerably more character to notice what is good in a
person and then actually say it to their face.

We want to be people who are generous with encouragement — who say the kind thing
while the person is still in the room, express gratitude while there is still
time, and assume the best before assuming the worst. Words shape homes, marriages
and children, and they last: long after somebody has forgotten exactly what
happened, they remember how what you said made them feel. That is a good enough
reason to choose them carefully.

---

Listening is one of the purest forms of love, and one of the rarest. Most people
listen only long enough to prepare their reply. Jesus asked remarkable questions,
not because He was short of answers but because He cared about the person
answering.

We would like to become slow enough to hear what somebody is actually saying.
Sometimes a person asks for advice when what they need is to be understood.
Sometimes anger is covering hurt, silence is covering fear, and laughter is
covering loneliness. Love pays attention long enough to notice the difference.

---

Forgiveness is one of the defining marks of discipleship, and one of the hardest
things in this book to actually do. There will be moments when somebody says
something that genuinely hurts, when trust has to be rebuilt slowly, and when the
apology comes late or not at all.

Forgiveness does not pretend those things did not happen. It does not remove
healthy boundaries or eliminate accountability, and it never requires anybody to
walk back into harm. It simply refuses to let resentment take up permanent
residence in our hearts. We forgive because we have been forgiven — again, and
again, and again.

---

Healthy relationships also require honesty, and honesty and love need each other
to stay upright. Love without truth collapses into sentimentality; truth without
love hardens into cruelty. Jesus Christ never asked anybody to pick one. He lived
both perfectly, which is why He is the standard rather than merely the example.

We hope to become people who speak truth kindly, refuse gossip, avoid
exaggeration, keep confidences, say difficult things gently, apologize
specifically rather than vaguely, and admit when we were wrong. Humility repairs
relationships far more often than pride ever has.

---

Not every relationship will become close. Not every disagreement will be
resolved, and not every act of kindness will be appreciated or even noticed. That
is all right. We are responsible for how we love; we are not responsible for
controlling how somebody responds to it. We choose kindness because it is who we
are becoming, not because it is guaranteed to work.

---

One day our children will leave home and build families of their own. Our
deepest hope is not that they remember the rules. It is that they remember how it
felt to be loved — the conversations around the dinner table, the bedtime
blessings, the laughter carrying through the house, the parents who apologized,
the siblings who eventually forgave each other, the neighbors who became friends,
and a home where people mattered more than perfection. If they remember that,
they will know how to build the same thing wherever they end up. And when their
own children ask them one day what matters most, we hope the answer is people,
always people, because every one of them is a beloved child of God.

> "Never let a problem to be solved become more important than a person to be
> loved."
>
> — President Thomas S. Monson,
