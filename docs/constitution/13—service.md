# The Birch Family Constitution

# Service

> "When ye are in the service of your fellow beings ye are only in the service of
> your God."
>
> — Mosiah 2:17

One of the truths we are most confident about is that God accomplishes an
enormous amount of His work through entirely ordinary people. He could send
angels more often and answer every prayer with an unmistakable miracle, and
sometimes He does. Far more often He sends a neighbor, a friend, a parent, a
bishop, a teacher, or a child — somebody willing to notice, willing to act, and
willing to love. We would like to be a family God can rely on to answer other
people's prayers.

---

Service is not something we get to after our own lives become comfortable, which
is fortunate, because that season shows no sign of arriving. It is simply part of
how discipleship is lived. There will never be a moment when everyone around us
stops needing encouragement. Somebody will always need a friend, or forgiveness,
or help carrying groceries, or a person willing to listen for twenty minutes. The
opportunities are endless — not because the world is hopeless, but because love
always finds somebody to bless. Extraordinary talents are not required for any of
it. Open eyes, open hands, and an open heart cover most of the job.

---

One of our mantras says **bless the one**, and it exists because the needs of the
world are genuinely overwhelming. There are too many problems and too much
suffering, and anybody who tries to solve all of it will be discouraged within a
week and useless within a month.

Jesus rarely ministered that way. He loved the multitudes and He continually
stopped for individuals — the woman at the well, the blind man, the leper, the
widow, the child. The one. We hope our children learn early that they are not
required to fix everything. They need to notice the person God has put in front
of them today. A single act of kindness rarely changes the whole world, and it
very often changes somebody's whole world, which was the assignment.

---

Service begins at home, and it is easy to miss that. It is wonderful to shovel a
neighbor's driveway; it is equally wonderful to empty the dishwasher before
anybody asks. We sometimes imagine meaningful service happens somewhere else —
across the world, at an organized project, on a mission — and those opportunities
are sacred. So is helping a younger sibling with homework, setting the table,
folding laundry, and listening patiently to somebody who has had a rotten day.
Home is where we build the habits that eventually go out into the world, and a
person who steps over their own family on the way to serve strangers has missed
the lesson entirely.

---

Not every act of service will be noticed, and that is part of the point. The
Savior specifically taught us not to go looking for credit. Some of the holiest
things any of us will ever do will never appear on a résumé: nobody may ever know
who cleaned the kitchen, wrote the encouraging note, quietly paid for somebody
else's meal, or prayed faithfully for a struggling friend for two years. God
knows, and that is genuinely enough. Service offered for recognition stops the
moment the recognition does. Service offered out of love becomes part of who a
person is.

We also hope to become people who can *receive* service gracefully. Pride
whispers that accepting help is weakness; the gospel says something different.
There are seasons when we carry somebody else's burden and seasons when somebody
carries ours, and both are gifts. Letting other people serve us is frequently how
they get to become more like Jesus Christ. Humility knows when to give, wisdom
knows when to receive, and grace makes room for both.

---

Serving will sometimes be inconvenient, and it is worth saying so plainly. The
phone rings at the wrong time. A neighbor needs help on the evening we had
plans. A child wants to talk properly just as we were heading to bed. A friend
asks for something when we are already exhausted. Love interrupts; it always has.
The Savior's ministry was full of holy interruptions, and again and again He
stopped, noticed, and blessed. We would like to become somewhat less protective
of our schedules and rather more attentive to people. Not every interruption is
from God — but a good many are, and we want the kind of hearts that can tell.

---

Another mantra reminds us that **we are His hands**, which is a comforting
thought and also a serious assignment. When somebody prays for comfort, perhaps
God sends the message we choose to write. When somebody prays for encouragement,
perhaps He sends the conversation we choose to start. When somebody prays for
hope, perhaps He sends a family willing to show up on a Saturday. We will
probably never know how often an ordinary act of kindness was the answer to
somebody's extraordinary prayer, which is exactly why small acts of goodness
should never be underestimated. The kingdom of God gets built quietly — one meal,
one visit, one invitation, one act of love at a time.

---

As our children grow, we hope they discover something the happiest people already
know: joy expands as love expands, and the more genuinely interested we become in
other people, the less trapped we are by our own worries. It is one of the
quieter miracles of Christian service that we set out to bless somebody and come
home having been blessed more. The Savior said it is more blessed to give than to
receive. That has always been true and it always will be.

---

If somebody describes the Birch family years from now, we hope one of the first
things they say is that we showed up. When somebody was grieving, when somebody
moved, when somebody was lonely, when there was work to do, when somebody needed
encouraging — we showed up. Not because we had endless time or because we were
extraordinary people, but because we believed loving our neighbor was one of the
clearest ways available to love God. May we become that kind of family, and may
our children spend their lives quietly becoming the answer to somebody else's
prayer.
