# The Birch Family Constitution

# Peacemaking

> "Blessed are the peacemakers: for they shall be called the children of God."
>
> — Matthew 5:9

Every family experiences conflict, and so does every marriage, friendship, ward,
team, and neighborhood. Wherever imperfect people gather for long enough,
disagreement follows. The question was never whether conflict would arrive at
this house. The question is what kind of people we become when it does.

We would like to become peacemakers, which is not the same as peacekeepers and
not remotely the same as people-pleasers. A peacekeeper avoids conflict at
whatever cost and calls the resulting silence peace. A peacemaker walks into
conflict carrying enough love, humility, courage, and patience to help something
heal. Jesus Christ never avoided a difficult conversation, and He never went
looking for one either. He told the truth, defended the vulnerable, forgave
generously, invited repentance, and brought peace wherever anybody was willing to
receive it. That is the pattern.

---

One of the more uncomfortable things we have learned is that contention is almost
never an accident. It is a series of choices — the words, the tone, the
assumptions, and above all the story we quietly tell ourselves about why the
other person did what they did. We do not always choose the disagreement. We
nearly always choose what we do with it.

President Russell M. Nelson taught that "Contention is a choice. Peacemaking is a
choice. You have your agency to choose contention or reconciliation." That has
become part of the working language of this family, because it puts the matter
where it belongs. In the middle of an argument we can still choose patience,
choose curiosity, pause before speaking, soften a voice, and ask one more
question instead of making one more accusation. Every one of those moves the
whole conversation.

---

When we are hurt, the instinct is to defend ourselves. Some of us get louder and
some of us get quieter; some withdraw and some attack; and most of us, at some
point, have privately concluded that if the other person would just admit they
were wrong, everything would be fine.

Experience says otherwise. Relationships rarely improve because somebody finally
won. They improve because somebody decided the relationship was worth more than
being right. That is not a claim that truth is unimportant — truth matters
enormously, and so do justice, accountability, and boundaries. It is a warning
that pride is skilled at disguising itself as principle. Before setting out to
correct somebody, it is worth asking a harder question first: **is my goal to be
understood, or to understand?** That one question has ended more arguments in
this house than any amount of being right ever did.

---

Listening may be the single most underrated act of peacemaking, because a
surprising number of disagreements continue for no better reason than that
neither person feels heard. Listening is not waiting for a turn to speak. It is
choosing to enter somebody else's experience with enough humility to let it
change what we think happened. It does not require agreeing with them; it
requires respecting them.

Sometimes another person's feelings rest on incomplete information. Sometimes
ours do. Sometimes neither of us is entirely right and both of us are partly
right, which is the most common outcome and the hardest one for pride to accept.
A humble heart makes room for that kind of complexity. It lets the other person
finish before assembling a defense, asks questions, and looks for understanding
before offering solutions.

---

Apologies are sacred, and a real one is one of the clearest expressions of
humility available to a person. It says: I was wrong, I hurt you, I'm sorry, and
I want to do better. Notice what it does not say — *if you hadn't*, *I'm sorry you
were offended*, *I guess*, *everyone makes mistakes*. Those are not apologies.
They are defenses wearing an apology's clothes, and children can identify them
instantly.

A real apology takes responsibility, acknowledges the hurt, and tries to repair
what can be repaired. We hope apologies become entirely ordinary here — not
because we fail more often than other families, but because we would rather not
let pride become more important than love. Often the strongest person in the room
is the first one willing to say "I was wrong."

---

Forgiveness is equally sacred and just as commonly misunderstood. It is not
pretending nothing happened, not forgetting, not removing every consequence, and
never returning ourselves to a situation that is harming us. Forgiveness is
choosing to hand resentment over to God rather than carry it, and refusing to let
somebody else's worst decision become the permanent shape of our own heart.

That kind of forgiveness is rarely instant. Sometimes it has to be chosen again
tomorrow, and again the day after, for a long time. The Savior understands that
road better than anyone, having walked it perfectly.

---

Healthy homes do not avoid difficult conversations; they get better at having
them. We hope our children grow up knowing they can disagree respectfully, ask
hard questions, express hurt honestly, set boundaries, receive correction, offer
it, and change their minds — all without anybody's worth being on the table.
Disagreement is not the opposite of love. Contempt is. The moment somebody stops
being our brother or sister and becomes our opponent, we have started walking
away from Zion.

Some conflicts will not resolve quickly. Some need time, prayer, distance, wise
counsel, and patience, and not every wound heals overnight. That is all right.
Healing is quieter and slower and much less dramatic than hurting, and it is
considerably stronger. We believe reconciliation is always worth pursuing when it
is safe and righteous to pursue it. We also believe some relationships require
firm boundaries before any peace is possible at all. Choosing peace does not mean
tolerating abuse, and choosing forgiveness does not mean abandoning wisdom.
Charity and discernment belong together; the Savior demonstrated both.

---

Few things test **Love Like Jesus** the way conflict does. Anyone can love people
who are easy to love; disciples learn to love people who are difficult.
Occasionally that difficult person is a stranger, more often a friend, quite
often somebody living under this roof, and sometimes it is ourselves. The
invitation does not change with the target: love anyway, speak kindly anyway,
forgive anyway, keep your covenants anyway.

If there is one thing we hope every Birch takes from this chapter, it is that
winning an argument has never once changed a heart the way love has. We would
rather be a family that apologizes than one that is always right, a family that
forgives than one that keeps score, a family that builds bridges than one that
builds walls. Long after every disagreement here has been forgotten, the love
will still be there. That is why we keep choosing it.

> "Charity is the antidote to contention."
>
> — President Russell M. Nelson,
> *Peacemakers Needed*, April 2023 general conference
