# The Birch Family Constitution

# Media

> "If there is anything virtuous, lovely, or of good report or praiseworthy, we
> seek after these things."
>
> — Articles of Faith 1:13

Every story is teaching something. Whether we are reading a book, watching a
film, listening to music, playing a game, or following somebody online, we are
letting another voice spend time in our minds, and voices that spend enough time
there eventually start to sound like our own thoughts. Over years, the stories we
consume shape the stories we believe about ourselves, about other people, and
about God.

That is why we choose media thoughtfully — not out of fear, which makes for
anxious families and rebellious teenagers, but out of stewardship. Our minds and
hearts are among the better things God has given us, and we would like to be
careful about what we fill them with.

---

Not all entertainment is of equal value, and we are not going to pretend
otherwise for the sake of sounding open-minded. Some stories lift our sights and
some slowly lower them. Some inspire courage; others make cruelty look clever.
Some awaken compassion; others get us laughing at things we ought to grieve over.
Some remind us that goodness is worth pursuing, and some quietly argue that
goodness is for the naive. The media we choose becomes part of the environment
our souls grow in, and just as good food strengthens a body, wholesome stories
strengthen a heart. Neither needs to become an obsession. Both deserve some
attention.

---

We hope this house stays full of stories, because good ones enlarge a person.
They teach us to imagine, help us understand people whose lives look nothing like
ours, and let us wrestle with difficult questions from somewhere safe. They show
us what courage, sacrifice, faithfulness, repentance, and hope actually look like
in practice, which is much harder to convey in a lecture. Some of the best
teachers our children will ever have never knew their names — they wrote books,
composed music, painted, and made films, and we are grateful for them.

Reading has a particular place here. Books ask something of a reader that most
entertainment does not: they ask you to participate, to slow down, to imagine, to
wonder, and occasionally to work. We hope our children become readers not because
reading is a school assignment but because books turn into lifelong friends. Some
entertain, some educate, and a few quietly change the person holding them.

---

Because stories matter, we protect them. One of the simplest ways to respect
somebody's experience is to refuse to spoil it — wonder is part of the gift,
discovery is part of the joy, and not knowing yet is part of the journey.

So when somebody asks us about a book, film, series, or game, we start by finding
out where they are in it. We do not run ahead, and we do not reveal a surprise
just because we happen to know it. Sometimes the most loving answer available is
"keep reading," or "you're asking exactly the right question," or simply "I don't
want to spoil that for you." Patience preserves wonder. We would like the Birches
to be known as people who protect stories rather than spoil them — and this
applies to our family's AI companion as firmly as it applies to an older sibling
who has already finished the series.

---

We also hope our children learn to think critically about what they are
consuming. Not every hero deserves admiration, not every popular opinion is wise,
and no message earns agreement merely by being repeated frequently. As disciples
we can ask different questions of a story: what picture of human nature is this
teaching, what does it celebrate, what does it mock, and what kind of person
would I become if I believed all of it?

Every author has a worldview, every filmmaker has assumptions, and every artist
tells us — deliberately or not — what they believe about life. We do not need to
reject every idea that differs from ours; that is not discernment, it is just
fear with better manners. We need to be able to recognize what is being argued
rather than absorbing it without noticing.

There will be times when we choose not to watch something, and not because we are
frightened of difficult ideas or think the world can be avoided. It is simply
that not everything deserves a place in our hearts. Some stories make it easier to
love what God loves; others make sin seem ordinary. Some cultivate gratitude and
some cultivate envy. Some invite the Spirit and some send Him away. We hope to
become people who notice the difference — not because somebody handed us a list of
rules, but because we have learned what light feels like when it arrives.

---

Media should also bring people together. Some of the happiest memories this
family makes will be built around stories shared out loud: reading before bed,
the same Christmas film every year, laughing through an animated one, music on a
long drive, and the conversation afterward about what any of it meant. Stories
get richer when they lead somewhere. We hope our children eventually realize that
the best part of family movie night was never the movie.

And there is wisdom in leaving room for silence. Not every spare moment needs
filling, and boredom is not an enemy — it is frequently the doorway to
creativity. Some of the best ideas anybody has arrive while walking outside,
building something, or sitting quietly with nothing scheduled. A life packed
entirely with noise leaves very little room to hear the Holy Ghost, and silence is
not empty. It is sometimes where God is easiest to hear.

---

As our children grow they will make more and more of these choices themselves,
and our real hope was never that they memorize a list of approved books and
forbidden films. Lists go out of date. Technology changes and culture changes;
the Spirit does not. We hope they develop hearts that recognize goodness, minds
capable of thoughtful discernment, and enough love of truth to be drawn naturally
toward what is virtuous, lovely, of good report, and praiseworthy. A person like
that can navigate a world we cannot predict.

Eventually the question stops being "what am I allowed to watch?" and becomes
"what kind of person is this helping me become?" That is the better question, it
is the Birch question, and we hope it goes on quietly echoing through every story
they choose for the rest of their lives.
