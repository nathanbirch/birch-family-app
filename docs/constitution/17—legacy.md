# The Birch Family Constitution

# Legacy

> "One generation shall praise thy works to another, and shall declare thy mighty
> acts."
>
> — Psalm 145:4

Every family leaves a legacy. The only real question is whether anybody chose it.
Some families pass down wealth, some pass down traditions, some pass down
stories, and some pass down wounds — but every generation inherits something from
the one before it, whether or not that inheritance was assembled on purpose.

What we hope to leave cannot be measured in dollars or kept in a safe. We hope to
pass down faith.

---

Our greatest desire is not that future generations remember our names. It is that
they remember the God we worshipped. Long after we are gone, we hope our children
and grandchildren and great-grandchildren know that Jesus Christ was the center
of this home — not because we were exceptional disciples, but because we kept
coming back to Him. We prayed, we repented, we forgave, we served, we kept our
covenants, and we tried again. That is the story we hope outlives us, and it is
the honest one.

We are grateful for the people who came before us. Some gave us faith, some gave
us opportunities, and some sacrificed for blessings they never got to enjoy
themselves. Some left examples worth following, and some left lessons we hope not
to repeat — and both of those are gifts, though only one of them feels like it at
the time. We honor our ancestors not by pretending they were perfect but by
gratefully receiving the good they handed us and improving what we pass on. That
is how a family gets stronger across generations.

---

Children learn far more from what they observe than from what they are told, and
this is the part of legacy that cannot be delegated. They notice whether we speak
kindly to each other, whether we pray when things are hard, whether we keep
promises and admit mistakes, whether we treat strangers with dignity, whether we
speak well of people who are not in the room, whether we love the scriptures,
whether we laugh, and whether we apologize. Long before our children remember any
advice we gave them, they will remember what we were like. Our lives are writing
the first chapters of their stories, and we are not going to get those pages
back.

---

We hope future Birches inherit more than photographs. We hope they inherit
stories: prayers that were answered, miracles that arrived quietly and were
almost missed, neighbors who became lifelong friends, difficult seasons that
turned out to have been building something, family vacations where everybody
laughed until it hurt, Christmas mornings, missionary farewells, temple sealings,
babies blessed, grandparents who knelt beside their beds, and ordinary Tuesdays
when somebody chose kindness instead of pride and nobody noticed but God. Those
stories become sacred because they are the evidence that He has always been part
of this family's journey.

We hope they inherit a reputation too — not for wealth or influence or
accomplishment, but for character. We would be content if people said that the
Birches keep their word, that they show up, that they work hard, that they are
kind to their neighbors, that they make room for people, and that they follow
Jesus Christ. If those are the things said about this family, we will have
received better honors than the world had on offer.

---

Legacy is built out of ordinary choices. Almost nobody wakes up one morning and
decides to create a remarkable family; remarkable families get built one
conversation, one dinner, one apology, one scripture study, one birthday, one act
of service, one kept covenant, and one encouraged child at a time. Most of what
shapes a family never makes any headline at all. It happens quietly and gets
repeated faithfully for years, which is how roots grow.

We hope every Birch understands that they are not only receiving an inheritance —
they are becoming part of it. Every generation gets to add something. Perhaps one
becomes known for generosity, another for remarkable faith, another for
courageous leadership, hospitality, scholarship, music, or simply for raising
children who love the Savior. Nobody is expected to repeat the previous chapter
exactly. We only hope each new one points toward the same Author.

---

This Constitution was never meant to preserve rules. It was meant to preserve a
way of life. There will come a day when the world looks very different from this
one: technology, jobs, schools, culture, and even some of our own traditions will
have changed beyond recognition. These principles should not have. Jesus Christ
will still be the Savior. Truth, kindness, integrity, and families will still
matter. The gospel will still be true, the Holy Ghost will still speak, the
scriptures will still teach, and love will still be the greatest commandment. A
generation holding on to those things will know how to navigate whatever world it
inherits.

---

Perhaps one day, many years from now, a young Birch will find this book on a
shelf — before a mission, or before a wedding, or in the first exhausted month of
being a parent. Perhaps they will smile at phrases they have heard their whole
life without knowing where they came from, and feel connected to people they
never got to meet.

If that day comes, there is one thing we would want them to understand. This book
was not written because we thought our family was exceptional. It was written
because we believe Jesus Christ is. Everything good in these pages belongs to
Him, and if these words help somebody love Him more, they will have done
everything we hoped.

May every Birch who comes after us know that they are loved — by their family, by
their ancestors, by Heavenly Parents, and above all by the Savior who made
eternal families possible. May they build on that foundation, strengthen it, and
leave it better than they found it.

> "Wherefore, be not weary in well-doing, for ye are laying the foundation of a
> great work."
>
> — Doctrine and Covenants 64:33
