# The Birch Family Constitution

# Repentance

> "Though your sins be as scarlet, they shall be as white as snow."
>
> — Isaiah 1:18

Few words in the gospel have been more thoroughly misunderstood than
*repentance*. To some it sounds like failure and to others like punishment, and
it is sometimes said with such heaviness that children learn to flinch at it.
That is a considerable distance from what it actually is.

We hope this family comes to understand repentance the way heaven does.
Repentance is not God's method of reminding us how often we fall short; it is His
method of helping us become who He made us to be. It is one of the greatest gifts
Jesus Christ has given the world, and the arithmetic of a world without it is
bleak — every mistake permanent, every regret a prison, every weakness an
identity. Because of Him, none of that has to be true. The gospel is not the
story of perfect people. It is the story of imperfect people who keep turning
back toward a perfect Savior.

---

Every disciple repents, and not because every disciple is unusually sinful. It is
because every disciple is growing. The closer anybody gets to Jesus Christ, the
more clearly they see how far there still is to go, and that recognition is not
supposed to be discouraging. Seeing more clearly means walking toward the light.

So we hope our children never imagine repentance is reserved for people who have
done something dramatic. It belongs to everybody: to children learning kindness,
teenagers learning integrity, parents learning patience, and grandparents still
learning wisdom. It belongs to anybody who sincerely wants to be more like Jesus
Christ, which in this house is meant to be all of us.

---

One of our mantras says **we start again today**, and those four words mean that
yesterday does not get the final vote. Perhaps yesterday was wonderful. Perhaps
it was painful, or we lost our temper, acted selfishly, and disappointed
ourselves badly. Today is still a gift. The Lord does not ask anybody to carry
yesterday indefinitely; He invites us to learn from it and begin again, and that
invitation is reissued every single morning. His mercies are new daily because we
need them daily.

---

Repentance requires honesty rather than perfection, and the two are frequently
confused. We cannot change what we refuse to acknowledge, which is why truth
matters so much in this family — truth is simply where healing has to start.
Sometimes that means admitting we were wrong, sometimes confessing something
difficult, and sometimes finally acknowledging a hurt we have spent months
working around. God already knows all of it. Repentance is not informing Him; it
is letting Him heal us through the telling.

We want this to be a home where honesty is met with love. Not a home where
consequences vanish, because consequences are part of how anybody learns
anything, but a home where the person is dealt with before the problem is. The
person comes before the problem here too, and never more so than when somebody
has just told us something hard.

---

There is an important difference between guilt and shame, and it is worth
teaching early. Guilt says *I did something wrong*. Shame whispers *I am
something wrong*. The first can lead a person straight to Jesus Christ. The
second persuades them to hide from Him, which is precisely why the adversary
prefers it — shame's entire argument is that you are beyond hope.

The Savior's invitation is the opposite. He asks us to come to Him exactly as we
are so that He can make us into something more. Nothing about repentance requires
pretending, and nothing about it requires despair. It requires trust: that Jesus
Christ is exactly who He says He is, and that He meant it.

---

We hope apologies become ordinary here — not forced, not rehearsed, and never
used as a tool to end an uncomfortable conversation early. An apology is one of
the simplest acts of courage available. *I hurt you. I was wrong. I'm sorry. Will
you forgive me?* Those four sentences have healed marriages, reunited families,
softened hearts, and rebuilt trust that seemed unrecoverable. We hope every Birch
becomes quick to say them and equally quick to accept them, not because either
comes easily but because both are Christlike.

---

Much of the time repentance is undramatic: a changed attitude, a kinder tone, a
better decision, a private prayer, a habit slowly replaced by a healthier one.
Not every miracle arrives with a bang. Some of God's best work happens so
gradually that it is only visible looking back across years — a child who has
somehow become patient, a parent who learned to listen before correcting, a
marriage strengthened by several hundred small forgivenesses nobody counted at
the time. Those quiet transformations are holy, heaven notices them, and so
should we.

---

Repentance also means extending grace to each other, because everyone in this
family is still in the middle of becoming. Mommy is becoming. Daddy is becoming.
Every child is becoming. None of us is in a position to demand perfection from
somebody else while depending so completely on the grace of Jesus Christ
ourselves.

That is not an argument for lowering standards. It is a reminder that
discipleship is a journey and people are traveling it at different speeds. Some
walk quickly, some slowly, some stumble often, and some are quietly carrying
something nobody else can see. We cannot always know what another person is
carrying. We can always choose compassion, which costs nothing and is almost
never wrong.

---

One day our children will be adults, and they will make mistakes we cannot fix
for them. They will know disappointment, heartbreak, failure, and regret. When
those days come, we hope they remember this chapter — and specifically that there
has never been a single day on which Jesus Christ loved them less because they
were struggling. Repentance is not the end of hope; it is where hope starts. It
is never too late to turn around, and the invitation has not changed since it was
first offered: come, begin again, walk with Me.

That may be the greatest miracle in the gospel. God does not merely forgive
people. He changes them — little by little, choice by choice, day by day, until
they look back and realize they have become somebody they could never have become
alone. That miracle is available to every member of this family, today and
tomorrow and for as long as any of us needs it. Which is why nobody here needs to
be afraid of repentance. We would rather be glad of it.

> "Daily repentance is the pathway to purity, and purity brings power."
>
> — President Russell M. Nelson,
> *We Can Do Better and Be Better*, April 2019 general conference
