# Editorial standards for the Birch Family Constitution

The Constitution is meant to be printed, bound, and handed to somebody who has
not met us. That is the standard every decision below is measured against. If a
sentence would embarrass us in a bound book on a grandchild's shelf, it does not
belong.

## The voice

It should read as though Nathan and Sarah spent years putting language around
the culture of their home — because that is what happened. It is warm, plain,
and unhurried. It says "we hope" far more often than "you must." It is willing
to be specific about ordinary things: a spilled drink, a Tuesday in Rexburg, a
sibling who took the good chair.

It is **not** a corporate values handbook, a policy manual, a parenting blog, a
motivational transcript, or a string of inspirational fragments. The reliable
tell for all five of those is the same one described below.

## Paragraphs — the rule that matters most

**Write in complete paragraphs.** Sentences that finish the same thought stay
together in the same block of text.

The single largest problem in the first draft was vertical fragmentation:
breaking a paragraph into a stack of one-sentence lines to manufacture drama.
It reads as urgent for about ten seconds and as exhausting forever after. It
also destroys argument — a paragraph can reason, whereas a stack of fragments
can only assert.

Do not write:

> People matter.
>
> Always people.
>
> Because love matters.

Write instead: *People matter more than the mess, more than the schedule, and
more than being right — because every one of them is a beloved child of God.*

A line standing on its own is earned only by a motto, a scripture, a short
quotation, or one deliberate moment of emphasis in a whole chapter. If a chapter
has more than two or three such moments, it has none, because emphasis that
happens constantly is just formatting.

## Patterns to avoid

These recur throughout the first draft and should be edited out wherever they
appear:

- **"Not this. This."** — *Not peacekeepers. Peacemakers.* Used once in a
  chapter it lands. Used six times it becomes a tic.
- **Escalating fragment lists.** *One prayer. One apology. One conversation.
  One ordinary day at a time.*
- **Stacked rhetorical questions.** Three or four in a row stop being questions
  and become a chant. Keep the best one; make the rest statements.
- **Anaphora by the dozen.** Long runs of sentences all opening with *We hope*,
  *Some*, *Every*, *It is*, or *Sometimes*. A short run is a figure of speech; a
  long one is a rut.
- **Trailing one-word affirmations.** *Always.* *Anyway.* *Together.*
- **Reflection questions at the end of a chapter.** This is a constitution, not
  a workbook. Every chapter ends on a statement, a scripture, or a verified
  quotation, and then stops.

## Quotations

Quotations are load-bearing, so they are rationed. Roughly one or two per
chapter in the body, plus the closing epigraph. A chapter that leans on five
quotations is borrowing conviction it should be supplying itself.

Every quotation must be verified word-for-word against a primary source —
`churchofjesuschrist.org`, `scriptures.churchofjesuschrist.org`, or
`speeches.byu.edu`. Never a quotation-aggregation site. The rule the app already
lives by in `src/config/mantras.ts` applies here in full:

> Putting words in a living person's mouth — or a dead prophet's — because they
> sounded about right is the one mistake this file must never make.

Three specific hazards, each of which the first draft actually hit:

1. **Attributing a speaker's quotation of somebody else to the speaker.** If a
   general conference talk quotes a bishop or a previous prophet, the words
   belong to the bishop or the prophet, and the talk is where you *found* them.
2. **Silent trimming.** Never elide with `...` inside a quotation in the
   published text. Either quote the whole sentence or start at a sentence
   boundary. If a trim changes a lowercase word to a capital, quote the fuller
   passage instead.
3. **Stitching non-adjacent sentences** into one quotation as though they ran
   together in the original.

Where the family has made a phrase its own — *bless the one*, *keep walking* —
that phrase is a **Birch family paraphrase**, and it is labelled as such rather
than dressed in quotation marks. See
[quotation-verification.md](quotation-verification.md) for the record of what
was checked and what had to change.

## Consistency

- **Jesus Christ** in full on first use in a chapter; *the Savior* and *Christ*
  thereafter. Never *Christ Jesus*.
- **Heavenly Father**, **Heavenly Parents**, **the Holy Ghost**, **the
  Atonement** — capitalised.
- **the Church** capitalised when it means The Church of Jesus Christ of
  Latter-day Saints; lowercase for a building.
- **Mommy** and **Daddy** — what the children call them, and what the
  Constitution calls them when speaking to or about the children.
- **general conference** is lowercase; talk titles are italicised.
- Serial commas. American spelling in the Constitution (the app's own developer
  docs use British spelling; the two are deliberately different audiences).
- The **em dash** is set closed, without spaces — like this.

## Structure

Each chapter opens with a scripture epigraph, gives its argument in flowing
prose, and closes with a verified quotation or scripture. Headings inside a
chapter are used only where the chapter genuinely has parts; otherwise the
prose carries itself with section breaks.

Chapters 01–05 are the foundation: who we are, what we are for, and the
language we use. Chapters 06–17 each take one strand of that language and
follow it out. Transitions should acknowledge the neighbouring chapters rather
than restating them — the reader is holding one book, not seventeen essays.

## Length

Depth, not word count. No chapter needs to reach a particular length to have
earned its place.

For the record, the first pass went from about 15,900 words to about 17,700 —
roughly 11% longer, which was not the expectation going in. Two things account
for it. Repetition genuinely came out, but joining fragments into paragraphs
costs connective tissue: a stack of six assertions becomes one sentence with
subordinate clauses, and that sentence is longer than any of the six even though
it replaced all of them. And chapter 05 nearly doubled on its own, from 826 words
to about 1,500, because every quotation gained its full verified wording, the
talk title, and the occasion — length that is doing real work and should not be
trimmed back.

The lesson for the next pass is that "shorter" was the wrong target. **Fewer
ideas per chapter, more thoroughly said** is the right one, and it is worth
checking each chapter against it rather than against a word count.

## What must survive every edit

Two things are non-negotiable, because they are the whole point:

**The Constitution is invitational.** It is a compass, not a weapon, and never a
measuring stick for deciding who is the good Birch. Nothing in it may be
quotable against a child.

**Worth is not performance.** No chapter may imply, even by omission, that a
Birch is loved in proportion to how well they are doing. That belief is the one
thing this book exists to prevent.
